# CS 250 Portfolio

Interpreting user needs begins by translating high-level business goals into actionable, value-driven software increments. In the SNHU Travel pilot, creating user stories with standard syntax and the INVEST guidelines allowed the team to focus directly on specific user outcomes—such as filtering travel options by price and schedule—rather than getting bogged down in static, upfront specification documents. A primary challenge in this phase was establishing and upholding a strict Definition of Done. By requiring automated unit testing, continuous integration, and peer reviews before accepting any increment, the team shifted defect detection upstream and ensured that each completed story delivered reliable, functional software.   

My approach to program development centers on iterative delivery, early risk mitigation, and regular feedback loops. During the project, our team navigated several common Agile hurdles, including the learning curve of relative estimation with Planning Poker and handling sudden technical dependencies without derailing in-flight sprints. In future projects, I plan to incorporate time-boxed architectural spikes to evaluate unknown technical risks, visual Kanban boards with work-in-process limits to expose bottlenecks, and MoSCoW prioritization to absorb shifting stakeholder requirements. Moving forward, I also intend to maintain the "adaptive chef" mindset emphasized in the project retrospective: understanding core development principles so I can tailor Agile or hybrid workflows to the unique complexity and regulatory demands of each system rather than blindly following a rigid template.   

Being an effective team member in software development requires cross-functional accountability, mutual trust, and transparent communication. Rather than working in isolated functional silos, great teammates share collective ownership of the product and actively remove friction for others. This was best demonstrated during the Daily Scrum, where members clearly articulated impediments—such as missing test datasets or external vendor delays—and resolved them immediately to protect the sprint goal. Fostering psychological safety allows teams to conduct honest, blameless sprint retrospectives, continuously refine internal processes, and take collective pride in sustainable software delivery.
